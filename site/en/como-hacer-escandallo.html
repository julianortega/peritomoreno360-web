Practical guide

How to cost a recipe step by step, with an example

A recipe costing sheet (in Spain, an escandallo) tells you how much it costs to produce one portion of a dish. Without it you don't know whether a dish makes you money, what you should charge for it or what happens to your margin when the price of oil goes up. Here is the full method, with an example from start to finish.

1 · What it is

What a recipe costing is

A recipe costing breaks a dish down into all its ingredients, with the quantity in each portion and the price of each one. The total is the cost per portion. With that cost and the selling price you get the dish's food cost and its margin.

A good recipe costing also includes what you don't see on the plate: the waste when preparing the product, sauces and sides, frying oil and, for delivery, the packaging.

2 · The method

The 6 steps to cost a dish

  1. List every ingredient in the recipe, including the small ones: salt, oil, spices, sauces, sides and packaging if it goes out for delivery.
  2. Weigh the quantity per portion exactly as it is served. "A handful" won't do: if the kitchen has no set weights, the costing is useless.
  3. Write down the purchase price of each ingredient per kilo, litre or unit, excluding VAT, using the supplier's latest price.
  4. Apply the waste. If you buy 1 kg of onion and have 850 g left after peeling, the yield is 85% and the real cost per clean kilo is the price ÷ 0.85.
  5. Calculate the cost of each line: quantity per portion × price per clean kilo. Add up all the lines and you have the cost per portion.
  6. Compare it with the selling price excluding VAT: food cost = cost per portion ÷ price excluding VAT × 100. The gross margin is the difference between the two.

3 · An example

Costing a burger

Example prices, excluding VAT. What matters is the method.

  • Minced beef, 150 g at €9.20/kg (100% yield): €1.38
  • Brioche bun, 1 unit at €0.42: €0.42
  • Cheddar cheese, 2 slices of 20 g at €8.50/kg: €0.34
  • Onion, 30 g at €1.10/kg with an 85% yield (€1.29/kg clean): €0.04
  • Lettuce and tomato, 40 g at €2.40/kg with an 80% yield (€3.00/kg clean): €0.12
  • House sauce, 25 g at €4.00/kg: €0.10
  • Chips, 180 g at €1.60/kg, plus frying oil: €0.35

Cost per portion: €2.75.

If you sell it for €13.50 including 10% VAT, the price excluding VAT is 13.50 ÷ 1.10 = €12.27. Food cost = 2.75 ÷ 12.27 × 100 = 22.4%. Gross margin: €9.52 per burger.

For delivery, add the packaging (for example €0.30) and, above all, the platform's commission. With a 30% commission on €12.27, you keep €8.59, and the margin drops to 8.59 − 2.75 − 0.30 = €5.54. That's why it pays to have one costing per channel, as we explain in recipe costs for dine-in and delivery.

The most common mistakes

What makes the costing say one thing and the till another.

01

Ignoring waste

Costing with the gross weight makes the dish look cheaper than it really is.

02

Old prices

A costing from six months ago is useless if the supplier has raised prices three times.

03

Forgetting the small things

Oil, sauces, bread, napkins and packaging add up to cents that, multiplied by a thousand portions, are real money.

04

Calculating with VAT

Food cost is always calculated on the price excluding VAT. With VAT the percentage comes out lower than it really is.

05

One costing for everything

Dine-in and delivery have different prices, packaging and commissions: they need different calculations.

06

No set weights in the kitchen

If every cook portions by eye, the costing is just theory. The recipe card has to be in the kitchen.

07

Doing it once and forgetting it

A costing is a living tool: review it every time a recipe or a price changes.

Frequently asked questions

What is a recipe costing in hospitality?

It's the cost sheet for a dish: all its ingredients with the quantity per portion, the price of each one and the waste. The total is the cost per portion, which you use to set the selling price and control the margin.

How is the cost per portion calculated?

By multiplying the quantity of each ingredient by its price per clean kilo (purchase price divided by the yield after waste) and adding up all the lines.

What is waste in a recipe costing?

It's the part of the product lost when cleaning, cooking or portioning it. It's measured as yield: if you use 800 g out of 1 kg bought, the yield is 80% and the real cost per kilo is the price divided by 0.8.

How often should recipe costs be updated?

Every time the price of an ingredient or the recipe changes. With an app that reads prices from delivery notes, like Flight Director, they update themselves.

Is the costing done with or without VAT?

Without VAT, both the purchase prices and the selling price you compare them with.
