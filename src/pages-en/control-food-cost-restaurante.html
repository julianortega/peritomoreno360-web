How to calculate and control a restaurant's food cost

Food cost is the percentage of each sale that goes on ingredients. Along with staff, it's one of a restaurant's two big costs, and one of the easiest to lose control of without noticing. Here we explain how to calculate it, what figure to aim for and how to keep it in check.

1 · The formula

How food cost is calculated

Food cost (%) = cost of ingredients ÷ sales excluding VAT × 100

It can be calculated in two ways, and it's worth using both:

  • Theoretical food cost: each dish's cost according to its recipe costing. It tells you what what you sell should cost.
  • Actual food cost: opening stock + purchases − closing stock, divided by the sales for the period. It tells you what it really cost you.

The difference between the two is your leak: waste, generous portions, breakages, ordering mistakes or food that gets thrown away.

2 · An example

Food cost of a dish

A dish sells for €12.90 including 10% VAT. The net price excluding VAT is 12.90 ÷ 1.10 = €11.73.

According to its recipe costing, its ingredients cost €2.84.

Food cost = 2.84 ÷ 11.73 × 100 = 24.2%.

Watch out with delivery: if the platform charges a 20% commission, the food cost as a share of the sale looks lower because the price is higher, but the margin you keep is smaller. That's why you need to look at margin per channel, not just the percentage. We explain it in recipe costing for dine-in and delivery.

3 · The benchmark

What is a good food cost?

It depends on the type of cuisine, the average spend and the product, but as a guide many restaurants work with a food cost of between 25% and 35%. Fast food and fast casual concepts tend to sit at the low end of that range; those with expensive products (fish, quality meat, seafood) at the high end.

More important than the exact figure is setting your target, measuring it every week and reacting when it drifts. One point of food cost in a venue selling €30,000 a month is €300 a month, €3,600 a year.

7 ways to control food cost

What we do every day in our own restaurants.

01

Up-to-date recipe costs

If supplier prices change and the costing doesn't, you're making decisions with false data.

02

Spec sheets and portion weights

A 140 g portion instead of 120 g is 17% more cost on that ingredient.

03

Frequent stock counts

Counting every week (or every day for expensive items) is the only way to know your actual food cost.

04

Record waste

What isn't written down doesn't exist. Logging what gets thrown away shows you where the problem is.

05

Prep just enough

Preparing according to forecast sales and how long each prep lasts avoids throwing food away.

06

Compare suppliers

Review prices regularly and check that each delivery note matches what you ordered.

07

Review the menu

High-cost, low-selling dishes are candidates for a price change, a new recipe or coming off the menu.

Frequently asked questions

What is food cost?

It's the percentage of sales excluding VAT that goes on paying for ingredients. It's calculated by dividing the cost of ingredients by sales excluding VAT and multiplying by 100.

Is food cost calculated with or without VAT?

Without VAT. VAT isn't income for the restaurant, so sales are always taken before tax.

What's the difference between theoretical and actual food cost?

The theoretical figure comes from the recipe costs of what you've sold; the actual one from the stock counts and purchases for the period. The difference reveals waste, uncontrolled portions and mistakes.

How often should it be calculated?

Ideally you should track it at least every week. With an app like Flight Director you have it every day.
